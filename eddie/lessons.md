# Eddie lessons (manually curated)

This file captures editorial calibrations across sessions — corrections [Your Name] has made to
Eddie's findings that should persist into future reviews. The eddie-second-eyes agent
reads this file and applies its rules when reviewing Eddie's findings.

**How to add a lesson:**
1. Run `/eddie` and review the output.
2. If a finding was a false positive AND the same false positive will recur on similar
   documents in the future, add a lesson here.
3. Eddie also auto-suggests lessons in the "Suggested lessons.md additions" section of
   each report — copy them here when they look right.

**What NOT to add:**
- One-off context errors (specific to one document)
- Lessons that contradict the senior-editor profile or banned-phrases list
- Lessons that [Your Name] isn't sure about — these create noise

**Format:** one bullet per lesson. Include date for traceability. Project-scoped lessons
go under "Project-specific" with the project name.

## Calibrations

  - "ensure" is acceptable when used in a quoted policy or contractual clause — don't flag as banned phrase.  (2026-05-10, validation)

  - Institute and center names with ampersands in their official logo/branding (e.g., "Institute for Law & Economics", "Center for Technology, Innovation & Competition") may be written with "and" in running prose. Flagging the ampersand vs. "and" choice as a factual error is house-style overreach unless the document mixes both forms.  (2026-05-10, PCL AI Initiative groundwork v2)

  - Pandoc `>`-prefixed bullets in markdown rendered from a `.docx` source are a rendering artifact, not a defect in the underlying document; do not flag unless the markdown itself is the deliverable.  (2026-05-10, PCL AI Initiative groundwork v2)

  - Internal cross-references like "see Building Tier 3 below" inside a short, single-author internal planning document with explicit tier/section headers are not orphaned references; the orphan-reference flag should require either an actual broken pointer or evidence the document is meant to be excerpted/forwarded.  (2026-05-10, PCL AI Initiative groundwork v2)

  - The "trailing summary" AI-tell pattern applies to bullet lists that restate just-completed prose. A closing deliverables register with explicit deadlines (e.g., "By Labor Day: what's in hand") is structurally load-bearing — it converts pillar-organized content into a deadline-organized deliverables list — and should not be flagged as redundant.  (2026-05-10, PCL AI Initiative groundwork v2)

  - In formal legal correspondence (recommendation letters to judges, formal client letters), closing offers of availability such as "Please do not hesitate to contact me if I can provide any further information" are an established convention and should NOT be flagged as banned phrases. The CLAUDE.md banned phrase "Please don't hesitate to reach out" targets the outreach-initiation register typical of business email, not the availability-for-follow-up register of formal letter sign-offs.  (2026-05-11, Threlfall rec)

  - In recommendation letters, a "two-year TA role" framing paired with "I asked her back for a second year, and would gladly have her for a third" is not a temporal contradiction. The first describes the cumulative relationship; the second describes the original asking-back decision plus a forward-looking endorsement. Don't flag this pattern.  (2026-05-11, Threlfall rec)

  - In [Your Name]'s clerkship A-letters, the doctrinal-anchor block's "course + grade + difficulty" parallel structure (first course + grade, then prior course + grade, then a progression sentence) is the prescribed template architecture (Recommendations project CLAUDE.md, A-letter block 2), not an AI parallelism tell. Don't flag matched grade-sentence structure across the two-course progression.  (2026-05-16, Vantrease rec)

  - In recommendation/formal letters built from an established template, the template's fixed opener/closer pair is a house-style arc, not a register mismatch with the body. Example: the [Your Name] B+ template opens "I am pleased to recommend X" and closes "I offer X a strong and enthusiastic recommendation"; the A template opens "I write with the greatest enthusiasm to recommend X" and closes "I recommend X to you with my highest enthusiasm and without reservation." Don't flag the closer as over-warm relative to a measured body when it matches the template's paired opener. Multiple single-context agents independently flagging the closer is a cross-agent bias pattern, not corroboration.  (2026-05-16, Brackenridge rec)

  - When a project designates the candidate's resume/cover letter as the primary source of truth (e.g., the Recommendations project), the letter tracking the resume's label for a credential is correct practice. A divergent official-transcript label for the same, substantively undisputed credential is a minor verification note (P3), not a P1 — do not escalate to P1 on source-tier divergence alone when the underlying substance is undisputed.  (2026-05-16, Brackenridge rec)

  - Do not infer the recommender's relationship scope from the transcript or resume ("only one course, no TA/RA, therefore the character/demeanor claims are unsupported"). Faculty routinely advise, mentor, and counsel students outside any enrolled course, and that contact appears on no transcript. Flag personal-quality claims as an exposure risk only when the document itself shows no plausible basis — not merely because the course record is thin — and confirm with the recommender before treating an out-of-class-knowledge claim as overreach.  (2026-05-16, Brackenridge rec)

  - In anonymized academic drafts, audit footnote/reference **keys** (not just the prose) for name- or example-derived tokens — a `[^surname]` or `[^distinctive-conceit]` key is a grep-able deanonymization leak even when the rendered prose is fully anonymized, because markdown→Word/PDF can surface keys as visible anchors and anyone sent the source file can grep them. Recommend renaming such keys to neutral tokens and auditing every key, not just the one caught.  (2026-06-04, Can AI Ace Your Exam — Eddie P1-4)

  - On personal/professional websites and bio pages written in the subject's own voice, confident first-person framing — soft quantifiers ("a lot of my time"), aspirational claims ("out in front of"), rhetorical anaphora ("real cases, real briefs, real oral arguments"), and hedged superlatives ("one of the most intensive") — is genre-appropriate and should not be flagged as overclaiming or AI-tell unless it makes a falsifiable factual claim. Calibrate against website-bio norms, not email/memo norms.  (2026-06-15, polkwagner.com homepage review)

  - In telegraphic talking-points / speaking-notes documents (bulleted internal notes a person reads aloud), soft closers ("and it's public", "and we'd like company") are speaker cues — beats to hit aloud — not delivered-prose filler; flag at most P4. Likewise, section-label prefixes ("Honest self-assessment:") are navigational cues for the speaker, not prose the audience hears — P4 at most, not P3.  (2026-06-25, COD AI Retreat talking points)

  - "Binding constraint" / "the constraint is X" is a precise economics term, not hedge or permission language, when the sentence then names X concretely (e.g., "the binding constraint is secure data access, not compute"). Don't flag as hedging overload.  (2026-06-25, COD AI Retreat talking points)

  - A deliberate antithesis placed as a spoken Q&A soundbite in talking points (a memorable one-line closer that also carries real content) is a legitimate rhetorical device, not the hollow-antithesis tell — calibrate against spoken-register norms; cap at P4 when the line also conveys substance.  (2026-06-25, COD AI Retreat talking points)

  - **In a memo that explicitly solicits comment, first-person hedges are the correct register, not AI tells — do not strip them.** In a *proposal* circulated to colleagues who have standing to object (and which closes by asking for their views), "I think," "we believe," "I would rather X, but this is a close call and I am open to argument," and "I have tried to balance this" are collegial posture, not weakness or machine-generated softening. Flat declaratives in that genre read as presenting a fait accompli and cost [Your Name] the room. Calibrate against *deliberative* memo norms, not the assertive-document norms the banned-phrases list is tuned for. Distinguish sharply from the genuine tell, which is **meta-signposting** — announcing the act instead of doing it ("One question I cannot answer yet, and it matters," "and it is reflected throughout," "One adjustment makes this work"). Those narrate the document rather than performing it and should still be flagged at full severity. The test: does the phrase state a *position* (keep) or announce that the writer is about to state one (cut)?  (2026-07-13, Fall 2026 1L exam schedule proposal — voice-style-checker over-flagged six of [Your Name]'s own first-person constructions in a comment-seeking memo; [Your Name] overruled and kept all six)

  - **CourtListener's `analyze_citations` reports FOUND for citations that do not exist. Treat FOUND as "this page exists in some case," never as "this case is real."** Three failure modes, all confirmed by direct test on 2026-07-21. (1) *Pincites resolve to the containing case.* An invented "612 F.3d 1188" returned FOUND because page 1188 falls inside *United States v. Irey*, which begins at 1160. Any fabricated cite whose page lands inside a real case's range will verify. (2) *The name cross-check has a blind spot.* The tool dedups by unique citation **string** before verifying, so a fabricated case name attached to a citation used correctly elsewhere in the same document is never checked — "Smith v. Jones, 383 U.S. 1 (1966)" sitting alongside a correct *Graham* cite to 383 U.S. 1 passed clean and unflagged. That is precisely the shape a hallucinated cite takes in a real draft. (3) *False positives on long captions.* A correct short-form cite (*Phillips v. AWH Corp.*, 415 F.3d 1303) was flagged at similarity 0.33 because CourtListener stores the full 15-word caption. **How to apply:** never treat a clean `analyze_citations` run as verification of a legal citation. Cross-check each case **name** independently — search `case_name` and confirm the reporter cite matches — and open the opinion for anything load-bearing. A WARNING is a question, not a verdict; the absence of a WARNING is not clearance. Note this calibration cuts opposite to most entries here: it guards against Eddie *missing* a fabricated citation, not against over-flagging.  (2026-07-21, CourtListener MCP reference build)

  - **Before flagging phrasing in a revised document, check whether it implements the previous Eddie review's suggested fix.** If it does, either clear it or state explicitly that the prior recommendation is being reversed and why. Silently re-flagging text the last review prescribed reads as the tool contradicting itself, and it burns the credibility of every other finding in the run.  (2026-07-21, JD Concentrations memos — three findings re-flagged language the 2026-07-20 review had recommended and [Your Name] had implemented)

  - **Label-dash bullets (`**Label** — text`) in timelines, definition lists, and deliverables registers are a list-formatting convention, not prose em-dashes.** Exclude them from em-dash density counts and don't flag them as an AI tell. CLAUDE.md prohibits em-dashes as bullet *leaders* — where `•` or `-` belongs — which is a different thing entirely.  (2026-07-21, JD Concentrations memos)

  - **When a document changes purpose across versions (design memo → approval proposal, project overview → framework request), test each dropped passage against the NEW document's purpose before flagging it as a plan omission.** Framing sentences, design-essay rationale, and deliberative asides are supposed to drop; operative rules, governance steps, and approval prerequisites are not. Check first whether the dropped content survives in a different and often stronger form — a numeric floor in a rule set can carry a guardrail that was a sentiment in the design memo.  (2026-07-21, JD Concentrations memos)

  - **Ordinary business and spatial idioms ("leaving value on the table," "on the shelf," "the road not taken") are not members of the hollow-antithesis or banned-metaphor families.** Those families are specifically: tidy two-part oppositions where the parallelism is the content, twee personification of abstractions, abstraction nouns standing in for the thing, dangling abstract verbs, announcing the act instead of doing it, and moralizing closers. Flag figurative language only when it substitutes for a mechanism the text never names — read the two adjacent sentences before flagging, because the mechanism is usually right there. Note that [Your Name] banned "load-bearing" as a metaphor on 2026-07-21; that ban is about *that phrase and its rationale*, not a licence to flag all figurative language.  (2026-07-21, JD Concentrations memos)

  - **Don't flag the precision of a derived estimate when the document, in the same paragraph, names the pending input and the person who will confirm it.** "Faculty get less than a week… Renata should confirm the January 2028 OCI dates" has already made the hedge; flagging the estimate duplicates it. Flag only if the estimate falls outside the range the pending input could actually produce.  (2026-07-21, Fall 2027 start-date memo)

  - **The moralizing-closer tell requires a *worse hypothetical* the text flatters itself against** ("a lesser analysis would stop here"). A closing clause naming a specific precedent the audience personally lived through — "which is the lesson of Fall 2026" — carries argumentative content and is not the tell. Don't flag.  (2026-07-21, Fall 2027 start-date memo)

  - **When checking a "most X" claim against a source giving per-instance counts, confirm the denominator matches the claim's unit before flagging.** A source saying "Monday carries about 45 upper-level courses" out of 159 total *course-days* does not put Monday at 28% of *courses* — courses meeting twice weekly are counted twice. Reconstruct the distinct-item count, then flag as unsupported rather than as false. Better still, replace the vague quantifier with the source's own number.  (2026-07-21, Fall 2027 start-date memo)

  - **When a document corrects someone else's arithmetic, audit its own arithmetic to the same standard before shipping.** Figures *inherited* from the document being corrected are the highest-risk class — the Fall 2027 memo corrected the Registrar's class-day row while quoting that same sheet's exam-day cell as fact, and that cell was also wrong. Also test whether the premise carrying the correction ("the published dates are self-consistent") actually survives; usually a narrower, evidence-backed route to the same conclusion exists and is stronger.  (2026-07-21, Fall 2027 start-date memo)

  - **Full-form terminology in prose paired with a consistently abbreviated form in tables is a layout convention, not terminological drift.** "Civil Procedure" in every prose sentence and "Civ Pro" in every table cell is a space-saving choice, not inconsistency. Before flagging, check whether the split is exceptionless across the document; if it is, clear it.  (2026-07-27, 1L calendar options memo)

  - **A project registry's record of *why* a decision was made does not constrain the rationale a memo states publicly.** Test the memo's stated reason on its own terms first. Flag a divergence only if the stated reason is false, or if the omitted reason is one the audience needs in order to evaluate the proposal. Internal deliberation is not something a memo owes its readers.  (2026-07-27, 1L calendar options memo — the memo defended a date choice on even spacing, which was true; the registry recorded a different true reason, and the divergence was flagged as an error)

  - **A zero-finding return from a single-purpose agent is not clearance when the agent's stated reasoning contains a verifiable factual error about the document.** Treat that pass as not run and re-execute it, rather than preserving the conclusion and discarding only the bad evidence. A voice pass that reports "no em-dashes" on a document containing four has demonstrated it was not reading the text, whatever its verdict.  (2026-07-27, 1L calendar options memo)

  - **When a document is revised by changing one figure across many cross-references, grep for the superseded value before shipping.** Three of one review's six P1s were a single morning's edit that took in six places and not in three — including the one place that named the operative dates. Consistency agents verify what they look at; they do not tell you what they skipped.  (2026-07-27, 1L calendar options memo)

  - **Registry support establishes a person's name and role — never that they performed the act the sentence describes.** A `NAMES.md` do-not-use row licenses a substitution and nothing else; applying the mapping and stopping yields prose that is name-correct and fact-wrong, and it reads as *verified* precisely because a registry was consulted. Check separately whether the corrected person actually did the thing; when they did not, the finding is a combined name-plus-attribution error, not a bare swap. The same rule governs the *fix*: "X holds the externship curricular portfolio" makes X the plausible candidate, and plausibility is exactly what fix-verification exists to catch — **drop the attribution rather than resolve it.** The struck name in the run that produced this *was* in the global roster; being registered was never the issue. Equally, **an unnamed fallback limb is not a fix — strike it.** "Remove the name, or replace with a verified faculty member" tells a downstream editor to supply a real person for a claim no source supports, converting a fabricated person into a real one wrongly credited — worse than the original error and much harder to catch, because the sentence now names someone who exists. Same for "attribute to a verified person and supply enrollment data." When removal is what the evidence carries, state removal as the sole fix. Like the CourtListener entry, this cuts against *missing* an error rather than over-flagging.  (2026-07-27/28, personnel-drift fixture — the unnamed-fallback limb fired three times in one run, more often than the upstream findings were wrong)

  - **Absence from an institution's faculty directory refutes a *faculty* claim and nothing more.** Word such findings "unverifiable as [institution] faculty," never "does not exist" or "fabricated" — real, currently-employed administrators are routinely absent from faculty directories. Also require that the null was actually tested: a directory search returning nothing is meaningless unless the same query returns hits for a name known to be listed. An untested null is not evidence, and a P1 built on one is a fabrication finding resting on a broken query.  (2026-07-27, personnel-drift fixture)

  - **A replacement figure drawn from a different unit is a new error, not a fix.** Before clearing a numeric correction, confirm the substitute counts the same thing as the original — swapping a placements-filled count for a unique-student headcount, or a seats-available figure for a positions-filled figure, yields a sourced-looking number that is still wrong, and the citation makes it harder to catch than the original. When the units cannot be reconciled, the correct finding is "unsupported — ask for the figure and its definition," not a substitution. Check the year too: the same number often appears in adjacent annual reports meaning different things.  (2026-07-27, personnel-drift fixture)

  - **Before flagging a percentage as non-derivable from a stated total, test the adjacent integer bases at the percentage's own precision.** "240 students, a 12% increase" is derivable: 214 → 240 is +12.1%, which rounds to 12%. An arithmetic finding that supplies its own counterexample is a false positive, and the residual concern (no baseline stated) is a different finding.  (2026-07-28, personnel-drift fixture)

  - **"Penn Carey Law" is the institution's own standard short form and is fine on first reference** in internal documents — do not require the full "University of Pennsylvania Carey Law School." Flagging it invents a house rule that does not exist.  (2026-07-28, personnel-drift fixture)

  - **In a short internal update, attributing a figure to the person or office that owns it is adequate sourcing.** Do not flag it as "unsourced" absent a reason to doubt the figure itself — if the figure is wrong, *that* is the finding; "who said it" is not a second defect. Likewise **"X's office" is acceptable shorthand** for a named person's organizational unit once their role has been given: do not require the unit be formally named, its staff enumerated, or a supervisor identified. Cleared on this fixture three runs running.  (2026-07-28, personnel-drift fixture)

  - **Before flagging "identical sentence template across paragraphs," check the syntactic position of the repeated element in each sentence.** A shared name-title appositive appearing as subject, then object, then participial modifier is not a repeated template — it is the ordinary convention for introducing people. The tell requires structural repetition, not a recurring *kind* of phrase. Watch for the self-contradiction that exposes this one: a report flagging three sentences as identical while separately flagging one of them for differing from the other two has refuted itself.  (2026-07-28, personnel-drift fixture)

  - **A rhetorical gesture at a body of law is not a citation-grade factual claim.** In guidance prose, "runs into ABA Standard 303, candor and competence obligations, and malpractice standards" names constraints bearing on a question; it does not assert that a codified rule resolves it. Flag such a limb only if the named body of law is inapposite, not because no single authority covers the exact application — and check whether the finding's own proposed fix (e.g. "cite Formal Op. 512, which names competence and candor together") in fact *supports* the flagged text. Three agents landing on one clause is a cross-agent bias pattern, not corroboration.  (2026-08-28, MIT report integration)

  - **On guidance pages and portals, an imperative sentence is not an assertion of institutional authority** when the surrounding page is already imperative ("Cover these seven points," "Do not add another task merely to detect AI"). Before flagging advice as reading like a mandate because of the author's title, read three sentences up and three down; if the register is established, clear it.  (2026-08-28, MIT report integration)

  - **Formative and summative assessment are not competing claims.** A required ungraded formative assessment (ABA Standard 314) and a final exam carrying all or most of the grade coexist by design. Do not flag "the final is the main event" as contradicting a formative-assessment requirement on the same page. Note the reviewer error that produced this: verifying that the 314 text *exists* was reported as verifying a *contradiction*.  (2026-08-28, MIT report integration)

  - **Third person is correct when reporting what a source concluded**, even on a page that otherwise addresses the reader as "you." "The committee's answer is that instructors should revisit…" is not the source's vocabulary leaking into house voice; converting it to "you" would put the source's recommendation in the author's mouth.  (2026-08-28, MIT report integration)

  - **Cite a PDF by its printed folio count, not its leaf count.** "(38 pages)" for a report whose last printed page is 38 but whose PDF opens as 40 is correct practice — front matter is unpaginated. "Correcting" it introduces a discrepancy with the document's own pagination.  (2026-08-28, MIT report integration)

  - **Two different denominators are not a contradiction — find the distinction before flagging.** "Sat for eleven finals" and "across ten graded exams" both verified true on the Spring 2026 exam study: eleven exams were sat, one was never returned. The right fix is to make the denominators legible ("ten of the eleven came back with results"), not to change a number. Check the study roster before reporting adjacent counts as inconsistent.  (2026-08-28, exam-taker study, per-exam-summary.csv)

  - **A page-marker page number from a document conversion is not a citation.** Docling's `<!-- Page N -->` markers track the PDF's own page labels and drift from the printed folio whenever there is unnumbered or roman-numbered front matter — two pages, on the MIT report. Verify every published page cite with `pdftotext -f N -l N` and read the folio off the page.  (2026-08-28, MIT report integration)

  - **`gh pr list`'s default output carries no column headers, and its trailing timestamp is `createdAt`, not `mergedAt`.** Verified against `--json` on four PRs, 2026-08-30: every displayed value matched creation exactly. The trap is that a PR merged soon after opening makes the two indistinguishable — three of the four agreed to the minute, and only the fourth, open eight days, exposed the error. Reading a merge date off that table put a wrong date in a project wiki and made a correct history entry look self-contradictory. **How to apply:** never state a merge date from the default table — ask for `--json number,mergedAt`. Generalize it to any unlabelled column in a CLI's human-readable output: with no header, nothing on screen will contradict the misreading, and a plausible-looking date reads as sourced. Like the CourtListener and Docling entries, this guards against *accepting* a wrong fact rather than over-flagging a good one.  (2026-08-30, Sixth Circuit portal PR reconciliation)

  - A completeness claim immediately followed by an explicit invitation to report exceptions ("live on every surface that matters… if you spot one I missed, post it here") is self-limiting, not an internal contradiction. Don't flag the pair, and don't propose softening a claim the text has already softened.  (2026-08-30, PCL AI Project rebrand Slack post)

  - A possessive already assigns credit: "Tom Baker's casebook is live with his students" does not read as the organization claiming his work. Flag announcement attribution only on a concrete ownership signal in the text — and never propose a fix that inserts an organizational claim ("his book, our pipeline") the original didn't make.  (2026-08-30, PCL AI Project fall reboot Slack post)

  - Applicant-facing hiring documents don't establish facts about existing staff. Before citing a posting, offer letter, or job description as evidence of a problem in a message to a current team, confirm the source actually covers that population — unresolved terms for new hires say nothing about people already on the roster.  (2026-08-30, PCL AI Project fall reboot Slack post)
