# [Your Name] Voice Profile

Use this profile as a calibration aid, not as a phrase-matching template. The goal is prose that sounds like [Your Name] in the relevant genre, not prose that performs a generic “professional” style.

## Core voice

- Lead with the point. Put the recommendation, judgment, or fact in the first sentence that can carry it.
- Use first person to own a judgment, recommendation, uncertainty, or request for comment. “I recommend,” “My read is,” and “I think” are often correct in a deliberative memo; do not flatten them into institutional passive voice.
- Prefer concrete institutional nouns and active verbs: faculty, students, Registrar, course list, vote, deadline, transcript, review, approve, record, defer.
- Be candid about limits. State observed facts directly; qualify the inference or prediction, not the observation.
- Sound collegial without sounding tentative. [Your Name] leaves room for disagreement when the audience has standing to object, but he does not apologize for having a view.
- Use a mixed sentence rhythm: a short sentence for the point, followed by a longer sentence when the qualification or mechanism matters. Uniform sentence length is a stronger machine tell than long sentences themselves.
- Use semicolons and em-dashes when they carry the relationship between clauses, create a useful pause, or preserve a compact qualification. They are part of [Your Name]'s normal prose; the problem is mechanical repetition, not the punctuation itself.
- Use an occasional plain-spoken turn: “The problem is,” “That is a real limit,” “The question is,” “My read is.” Do not repeat the same turn mechanically.
- Let a paragraph end on its consequence, decision, or next step. Do not end by congratulating the analysis or recapping the paragraph.

## Genre calibration

### Faculty and administrative memos

Open with the situation and the ask. Use “I recommend,” “I propose,” or “I ask the Committee” when [Your Name] owns the proposal. A memo seeking discussion may use first-person hedges and concessions; that is collegial posture, not weakness. Make authority boundaries explicit: distinguish what [Your Name] recommends, what a committee decides, and what another office controls.

### Emails

Open with substance. Contractions are natural but not mandatory in every message. Match the greeting and sign-off to the relationship. A short internal note may have no greeting; a faculty-wide message may need one. Do not impose memo headings on a two-paragraph email.

### Academic writing

Allow heavier parentheticals, semicolons, carefully chosen em-dashes, and longer sentence architecture when they carry an argument. Use a committing opening, a real concession-and-turn where warranted, and a closing sentence with a verdict rather than a section recap. Do not force memo brevity onto scholarship.

## High-value AI tells

Flag these when they are present and doing no real work:

- Procedural throat-clearing: “This memo will discuss…,” “The purpose of this section is…,” or a research-method opener before the actual finding.
- Meta-signposting: announcing that the writer is about to be clear, precise, balanced, or comprehensive instead of stating the point.
- Generic connective tissue: “taken together,” “in this context,” “against this backdrop,” “it is worth noting,” or “the larger point is” when the next sentence could simply begin with its conclusion.
- Smooth but empty symmetry: “not only X but also Y,” “both A and B,” or “from X to Y” when the parallelism replaces a mechanism, example, or consequence.
- Abstract nouns that hide the actor or action: “implementation,” “facilitation,” “leveraging,” “operationalization,” “stakeholder engagement,” or “the landscape,” when a person, office, decision, or event can be named.
- Lists of three or four near-synonyms that create the appearance of coverage without adding distinctions.
- Paragraphs that all have the same shape: topic sentence, three balanced sentences, summary sentence.
- Reflexive qualification: “may,” “might,” “could,” “arguably,” or “it is possible” attached to an observed fact rather than to a prediction or inference.
- False precision: an exact count, date, or characterization included because it sounds authoritative but is not supported or needed.
- Defensive completeness: a paragraph that anticipates every conceivable objection after the decision has already been made.
- Trailing recap: a final paragraph or list that repeats the body without adding a decision, implication, or next step.

## Do not flag as AI tells by themselves

- First-person judgment in a memo that solicits comment.
- “I think,” “my read,” “I would recommend,” or “I am open to” when they accurately mark the writer’s position.
- A single “but,” “and yet,” or “to be sure” that performs a real concession or turn.
- Parallelism in a genuine list, rule, comparison, or doctrinal test.
- Information-bearing parentheticals, footnotes, or qualifications.
- A short sentence after a long one, or a long sentence with real clause structure.
- Semicolons and em-dashes used to connect genuinely related thoughts. Do not use a universal numerical threshold; flag only punctuation that is repetitive, ornamental, or obscures the sentence.
- Conventional memo sections, an action list, or a conclusion that states what happens next.
- Plain, functional prose merely because it is not distinctive. Flag only when it is padded, generic, or mismatched to the author’s voice.

## Review test

For each proposed finding, answer all four questions:

1. What is the exact phrase or structure?
2. What does it make the reader misunderstand, distrust, or work too hard to find?
3. What would [Your Name] say instead in this genre and to this audience?
4. Is the proposed fix more natural, or is it merely more “professional” sounding?

If the answer to question 2 is only “I prefer a different style,” do not report the finding. If the issue is real, give a replacement that names the actor, action, fact, or consequence.
